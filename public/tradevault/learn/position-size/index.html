How to calculate your position size

Your position size should come from your stop, not from how sure you feel. Decide how much of your account you’ll risk, measure the stop, and the maths gives you the size. Here’s how, with a calculator.

By The Trade VaultUpdated 17 Nov 2026
Key takeaways
  • Risk amount = account balance × risk % (many traders use 0.5–1%).
  • Position size = risk amount ÷ (stop distance × value per point for 1 lot).
  • A wider stop means a smaller size, so the money at risk stays the same.
  • Contract sizes differ between brokers: check yours before you trust any number.

The position size formula

Every position size calculation uses the same three inputs:

  1. Risk amount: how much money you’re willing to lose on this trade. Account balance × risk %. On a $10,000 account at 1%, that’s $100.
  2. Stop distance: how far your stop loss is from your entry, in points or pips.
  3. Value per point: how much one point (or pip) is worth for 1 lot on your broker.

Position size (lots) = risk amount ÷ (stop distance × value per point per lot)

Check “value per point for 1 lot” in your broker’s contract specification. Common examples: US30 $1 or $10, gold $100 per $1 move, EUR/USD $10 per pip.

Example: US30

Account $10,000, risking 1% = $100. Your stop is 40 points away. $100 ÷ 40 points = $2.50 per point.

Now check your broker’s contract. If 1 lot of US30 is worth $1 per point, you’d trade 2.5 lots. If it’s $10 per point, you’d trade 0.25 lots. Same risk, very different lot sizes, which is exactly why you can’t copy someone else’s lot size.

Example: gold (XAU/USD)

On many brokers 1 lot of gold is 100 ounces, so a $1 move in the gold price is worth $100 per lot. Risking $100 with a $4 stop: $100 ÷ ($4 × $100) = 0.25 lots. Some brokers use different contract sizes, so confirm it in your platform’s contract specification.

Example: forex

For pairs quoted in US dollars, like EUR/USD, a standard lot (100,000 units) is worth about $10 per pip. Risking $100 with a 20-pip stop: $100 ÷ (20 × $10) = 0.5 lots. For other pairs the pip value changes with the exchange rate.

Rules that keep sizing honest

  • Pick one risk % and stick to it. Changing it after a loss is how small drawdowns turn into big ones.
  • Set the stop first, then size. Never shrink the stop to “fit” a bigger size.
  • Round down, not up. If the maths says 0.27 lots, trade 0.25.
  • Count your open risk. Three trades at 1% each is 3% at risk at once.
Leverage magnifies both ways.Leveraged products can lose you more than you deposit. Position sizing is how you keep each loss small and known.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I risk per trade?

Many traders risk between 0.5% and 1% of their account per trade, so a losing streak doesn’t do serious damage. There’s no single right number, but it should be small and consistent.

What is a lot in trading?

A lot is the standard unit of size for a trade. In forex a standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency; for gold and indices the size of 1 lot depends on your broker’s contract specification.

Why does my stop distance change my lot size?

Because the money at risk should stay the same. A wider stop means each lot loses more before the stop is hit, so you trade fewer lots.

Where do I find the value per point for my broker?

In MetaTrader, right-click the symbol and open its specification to see the contract size and tick value. Your broker’s website lists it too.
